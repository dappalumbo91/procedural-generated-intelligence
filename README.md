# Procedural Generated Intelligence

A desktop project for an intelligence that unfolds from compact rules, seeds, and composition functions, the way *No Man's Sky* unfolds a planet from a seed.

The founding brief is [CONCEPT.md](CONCEPT.md). The reaching goal is [GOAL.md](GOAL.md). What the kernel holds, and the results through this slice, are in [RESULTS.md](RESULTS.md). The runnable kernel is `pgi`, version `pgi-0.52.0`.

## What this folder stores

Generative rules, latent seeds, constraint grammars, and context-driven synthesis functions. A prompt arrives, and thoughts, reasoning chains, and world models unfold from those.

Large language model design and other artificial intelligence systems are reference points while this is built. The working shape is a hybrid: compact learned modules supply intuition and representations, and procedural rule engines handle structure, verification, and on-demand expansion.

## Layers

1. Seed and context anchoring
2. Layered generative rules: base, domain, detail
3. Constraint solvers and co-occurrence filters
4. On-demand expansion

Opened 2026-11-24. Short name: PGI.

## Kernel

`pgi` anchors a seed, saturates the active rule packs, then retracts any contradictory claims and the claims that rest on them. Rendering is a separate zoom. The claim set is already fixed before the outline is drawn.

- `pgi/math/` is the kernel, version `pgi-0.52.0`. A theorem is built only by a checked rule. The integer laws are the ring shared by Lean 4.36, Isabelle2025-2 HOL, and Coq's ZArith: equality, congruence, commutativity, associativity, identities, inverses, and distributivity. Integer order adds reflexivity, transitivity, antisymmetry, addition on the left, `Int.le_of_eq`, a true numeral comparison, and the negation of a false one. A compound side is compared only after those equality rules reduce it.
- Closed integer expressions reduce by those ring laws. Non-negative addition and subtraction also reconstruct the result from the digits, and the kernel keeps the step only when that reconstruction agrees with the ring operation. `2 + 3 * 4` is 14. `17 + 13 + 1` is 31. An exact integer quotient stays on that path.
- Closed rationals are reduced fractions. The denominator is positive and the numerator carries the sign. Addition, subtraction, multiplication, negation, and division use the Lean `Rat` operations and the Isabelle `Rat.thy` lemmas, then reduce. Equality is Isabelle `eq_rat`. Comparison is Isabelle `le_rat`. A fraction that reduces to denominator 1 is an integer. `8 / 2` is 4. `8 / 3` is 8/3. `1/2 + 1/3` is 5/6. `8 / 0` stays open.
- The continuum is the Cauchy quotient of the rationals from Isabelle `Real.thy`. A constant sequence is a rational. A positive square root is the bisection sequence whose width halves at every step. Square factors move into the coefficient. `sqrt(4)` is 2. `sqrt(2)` is the class `cauchy(sqrt(2))`. `sqrt(8)` is `2 * sqrt(2)`. `sqrt(2) * sqrt(2)` is 2. Comparison uses a strict rational enclosure of that sequence. A nested radical of one square-free integer is a tower `base^(odd/2^k)`. `sqrt(sqrt(2))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(2)))`, and its square is `sqrt(2)`. `sqrt(3 + 2*sqrt(2))` is `cauchy(1 + sqrt(2))` by `real_sqrt_unique`. `sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))` is `cauchy(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)))` by the same rule, and its square is `2 + sqrt(2)`. `sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(3))` is `cauchy(1/2 * sqrt(-2 + 2 * sqrt(3)) + 1/2 * sqrt(2 + 2 * sqrt(3)))` by the same rule, and its square is `sqrt(2) + sqrt(3)`. A negative radicand stays open. A sum of two different square roots stays that sum. `sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))` by the same rule, and its square is `sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)`. Its inverse is `1/3 * sqrt(-3 * sqrt(2) + 3 * sqrt(5))`. `sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))))` by the same rule, and its square is `sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))`. Its inverse is `sqrt(sqrt(20 + 14 * sqrt(2))) - 1/2 * sqrt(2 * sqrt(20 + 14 * sqrt(2)))`. `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))` by the same rule, and its square is `sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))`. Its inverse is `1/3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(-3 * sqrt(2) + 3 * sqrt(5)))`. `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))))` by the same rule, and its square is `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))`. Its inverse is `1/3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(-3 * sqrt(2) + 3 * sqrt(5))))`. `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))))` by the same rule, and its square is `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))`. Its inverse is `1/3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(-3 * sqrt(2) + 3 * sqrt(5)))))`. `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))))))` by the same rule, and its square is `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))))`. Its inverse is `1/3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(-3 * sqrt(2) + 3 * sqrt(5))))))`. `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))))))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))))))` by the same rule, and its square is `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))))`. Its inverse is `1/3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(3 * sqrt(-3 * sqrt(2) + 3 * sqrt(5)))))))`. `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))))))` is that nested radical by `real_sqrt_unique`, and its square is `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))))))`. Every further finite square root of this radical is the same rule, one square root deeper. Every further finite square root of one positive binomial nest is the same nested radical, one square root deeper. `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))))` is that nested radical by `real_sqrt_unique`, and its square is `sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)))`. Its inverse is `sqrt(sqrt(2 * sqrt(676 + 478 * sqrt(2)))) - 1/2 * sqrt(2 * sqrt(2 * sqrt(676 + 478 * sqrt(2))))`. The inverse of `sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(3))` is `-1/2 * sqrt(-2 + 2 * sqrt(3)) + 1/2 * sqrt(2 + 2 * sqrt(3))`. `sqrt(1 + sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))` is `cauchy(sqrt(1 + sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))` by the same rule, and its square is `1 + sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)`. Its inverse is the root times the inverse of that square. When the field norm is a rational square and the relative denesting succeeds, the root is written in the field: `sqrt(8 + 2 * sqrt(2) + 2 * sqrt(5) + 2 * sqrt(10))` is `1 + sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)`. The same family closes `sqrt(1 + sqrt(2) + sqrt(3))`. A nest times a pair is the square root of the product of those insides: `sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)) * sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))` is `cauchy(sqrt(2 + 2 * sqrt(2) + 2 * sqrt(5) + sqrt(10)))` by `real_sqrt_mult` and the same uniqueness rule. The inverse of a rational plus one admitted radical is the cofactor times the inverse of the difference of those squares, when the constructed quotient multiplies back to 1. `1 / (1 + sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))))` is `cauchy(1 - sqrt(2) + sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)) - sqrt(4 + 2 * sqrt(2)) - sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))) + sqrt(2 * sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))) - sqrt(sqrt(20 + 14 * sqrt(2))) + sqrt(2 * sqrt(20 + 14 * sqrt(2))))`. The same rule closes `1 / (1 + sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))`, `1 / (1 + sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))`, `1 / (1 + sqrt(1 + sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))`, and `1 / (1 + sqrt(2 + 2 * sqrt(2) + 2 * sqrt(5) + sqrt(10)))`. A fold times a nest on the same radicand is the square root of that mixed product: `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)))) * sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(2 * sqrt(58 + 41 * sqrt(2)))))`. A peak times a pair, or a peak times a high, on the same two radicands, is written the same way through the already closed high-times-pair and high-times-high products: `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))) * sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(229 * sqrt(2) + 145 * sqrt(5)))))`, and `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))) * sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(17 * sqrt(2) + 11 * sqrt(5)))))`. The shifted inverse then closes `1 / (1 + sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5)))))`. A deep times a fold is the same mixed product already used for a fold times a deep, so the constructed inverse of a rational plus a fold multiplies back to 1: `1 / (1 + sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)))))` is `cauchy(1 - sqrt(2) + sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)) - sqrt(4 + 2 * sqrt(2)) + sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))) - sqrt(2 * sqrt(2 + sqrt(2))) + sqrt(sqrt(20 + 14 * sqrt(2))) - sqrt(2 * sqrt(20 + 14 * sqrt(2))) - sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)))) + sqrt(2 * sqrt(sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)))) - sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(20 + 14 * sqrt(2)))) + sqrt(2 * sqrt(sqrt(20 + 14 * sqrt(2)))) - sqrt(sqrt(2 * sqrt(58 + 41 * sqrt(2)))) + sqrt(2 * sqrt(2 * sqrt(58 + 41 * sqrt(2)))) - sqrt(sqrt(2 * sqrt(676 + 478 * sqrt(2)))) + sqrt(2 * sqrt(2 * sqrt(676 + 478 * sqrt(2)))))`. Further finite depths of that fold close by the same rule. A crest times a pair, a high, or a peak on the same two radicands is written the same way through the already closed peak products: `sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))) * sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))` is `cauchy(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(40681 * sqrt(2) + 25729 * sqrt(5))))))`. The shifted inverse then closes `1 / (1 + sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(sqrt(2) + sqrt(5))))))`, and the quotient multiplies back to 1. A radicand the kernel cannot see as nonnegative stays open. Division by another element of this span multiplies by the inverse. `sqrt(2)/sqrt(3)` is `1/3 * sqrt(6)`, and `1/(1+sqrt(2))` is `-1 + sqrt(2)`. A product of radicals at different levels is one integer to the power `1/2^k`. `sqrt(2)*sqrt(sqrt(3))` is `sqrt(sqrt(12))`, and `sqrt(sqrt(2))/sqrt(sqrt(3))` is `1/3 * sqrt(sqrt(54))`. A divisor the kernel cannot see as nonzero stays open. Core Lean 4.36 has no `Real`.
- A finite set is an enumeration of those reals, built by Isabelle `empty` and `insert`. Membership, union, intersection, inclusion, and extensional equality compare normal forms. `{2, 2, 1}` is `{1, 2}`. `{1, 2} union {2, 3}` is `{1, 2, 3}`. The least upper bound of a nonempty finite set is its maximum: `sup {1, sqrt(2)}` is `sqrt(2)`, and `sup {1, 2}` is 2. Isabelle `complete_real` is that statement for an arbitrary nonempty set that is bounded above. The arbitrary case stays open. The empty set has no least upper bound in that statement. Core Lean 4.36 has `EmptyCollection`, `Membership`, `Insert`, `Union`, `Inter`, and `Singleton`, and it has no `Set` or `Finset`.
- A bounded quantifier is Isabelle `Ball` or `Bex` over a finite enumeration. `all x in {1, 2}. x <= 2` holds. A failing universal names its first counterexample. An existential names a witness. The empty universal holds and the empty existential fails, and that body is not evaluated. `prove complete {1, sqrt(2)}` states the finite least-upper-bound claim, with witness `sqrt(2)`. An unbounded quantifier stays open except for reflexivity, a closed equality witness, and leastness when the bound is a member. A bound strictly above the maximum is refuted at that maximum. The proof of `complete_real` for an arbitrary set stays outside this rule. A comprehension is a separate primitive. Core Lean 4.36 resolves `Exists`. `forall` is a keyword there.
- A comprehension is Isabelle `Collect`. Separation `{x in {1, 2, 3}. x <= 2}` is the finite subset `{1, 2}`. The empty domain is empty, and that body is not evaluated. `{x. x = 2}` and `{x. sqrt(2) = x}` are the singletons of those closed terms, by `singleton_conv` and `singleton_conv2`. Membership in an unbounded comprehension is the predicate at that element. Two unbounded comprehensions with the same predicate are one set, by `Collect_cong`. An order ray is a separate primitive. Core Lean 4.36 does not resolve `Collect`, `Set.sep`, or `setOf`.
- An arbitrary set is an order ray or the set of every real. `{x. x <= 2}` is `atMost(2)`. `{x. sqrt(2) <= x}` is `atLeast(sqrt(2))`. `{x. x = x}` and `{x. x <= x}` are `univ`, the set of every real. Inclusion follows the bounds by `atMost_subset_iff` and `atLeast_subset_iff`. A ray is not a finite enumeration, by `infinite_Iic` and `infinite_Ici`. `{x. x <= 2}` intersect `{x. 2 <= x}` is the singleton `{2}`. A proper overlap of a lower ray and an upper ray is the two-sided interval. `{x. 1 <= x}` intersect `{x. x <= 3}` is `atLeastAtMost(1, 3)`, and its supremum is 3 by `cSup_atLeastAtMost`, because the upper bound is a member. `{x. 1 <= x}` intersect `{x. x < 3}` has the same supremum by `cSup_atLeastLessThan`: the upper bound is not a member, and the kernel builds it by the Archimedean bisection. `sup {x. x <= 5}` is 5, and `prove complete {x. x <= 5}` is the least-upper-bound claim for that down-ray, by `cSup_atMost`. `{x. x < 5}` is `lessThan(5)`. The bound is not a member. `sup {x. x < 5}` is 5, and `prove complete {x. x < 5}` is the least-upper-bound claim for that open ray: the kernel runs the Archimedean bisection in the proof of `complete_real`, and `cSup_lessThan` names the result. An up-ray, open or closed, is not bounded above. A predicate that is not a lessThan ray or an interval with an open upper end stays outside that bisection. `{x. 1 <= x and x <= 3}` is that same closed interval, by `Collect_conj_eq`. The complement of an order ray is the opposite ray, by `Collect_neg_eq`. A nested conjunction is associated and met, so `{x. 1 <= x and x <= 3 and x <= 4}` is `atLeastAtMost(1, 3)`. A disjunction of two closed equalities is their union, by `Collect_disj_eq`. A disjunction of two rays is one admitted set when they overlap or touch: `{x. x <= 1 or x <= 3}` is `atMost(3)`, and `{x. x <= 3 or 1 <= x}` is `univ`. A union that leaves a gap is stored as those pieces in order: `{x. x <= 1 or 3 <= x}` is `atMost(1) union atLeast(3)`. The complement of a finite set is the open rays and open intervals around its members: `{x. not x = 2}` is `lessThan(2) union greaterThan(2)`. `not not` is `double_compl`, so `{x. not not x <= 2}` is `atMost(2)`. `imp_conv_disj` rewrites implication: `{x. x <= 1 implies x <= 3}` is `univ`. A comparison that is affine in the binder cancels to a standard ray: `{x. x <= x + 1}` is `univ`, `{x. 2 * x <= x + 1}` is `atMost(1)`, and `{x. 2 * x = x + 1}` is `{1}`. An identically false affine comparison is empty. A quadratic comparison is settled by its sign chart at the roots the kernel can construct and check: `{x. x * x <= 2}` is `atLeastAtMost(-sqrt(2), sqrt(2))`, and `sup {x. x * x <= 2}` is `sqrt(2)`. `>=` and `>` are rewritten to `<=` and `<` by flipping the sides, by `ge_iff_le` and `gt_iff_lt`, so `{x. x * x >= 2}` is `atMost(-sqrt(2)) union atLeast(sqrt(2))`. A comparison that is a polynomial in the binder, with rational or algebraic coefficients, is settled by one checked sign chart. When a coefficient is a tower element or a real algebraic number, a resultant (the field norm, or an eliminant that is a multiple of the norm) reduces the polynomial to a rational one. Square-free factoring and a Sturm sequence isolate the candidate real roots. A candidate is kept only when an exact check shows it vanishes in the original polynomial, and intervals are refined until every sign is certain. A root that is a nested square root is kept in that form. Otherwise the root is a real algebraic number: a primitive irreducible integer polynomial together with the index of that real root. `{x. x * x * x <= 2}` is `atMost(root(x^3 - 2, 1))`. `{x. x * x * x * x <= 2}` is `atLeastAtMost(-sqrt(sqrt(2)), sqrt(sqrt(2)))`. `{x. (x * x * x) - x <= 0}` is `atMost(-1) union atLeastAtMost(0, 1)`. `sup {x. x * x * x <= 2}` is `root(x^3 - 2, 1)`. `{x. x * x <= sqrt(sqrt(2)) + sqrt(sqrt(3))}` is `atLeastAtMost(root(x^32 - 20 * x^24 - 666 * x^16 - 3860 * x^8 + 1, 1), root(x^32 - 20 * x^24 - 666 * x^16 - 3860 * x^8 + 1, 4))`. `{x. x * x * x <= sqrt(2)}` is `atMost(root(x^6 - 2, 2))`. `{x. (x * x - 2) * (x - sqrt(2)) <= 0}` is `atMost(-sqrt(2)) union set({sqrt(2)})`. Core Lean 4.36 does not resolve `Set.Iic`, `Set.Ici`, `Set.Iio`, or `Set.univ`.
- GNU Octave cross-checks closed integers that fit in the exact range of an IEEE double. The binary is taken from `PGI_OCTAVE`, then `octave-cli` on PATH, then the Windows 11.3.0 install path used on this project's machine. The cross-check is skipped when none of those is found. Octave is not used for rationals, reals, sets, quantifiers, comprehensions, order rays, intervals, or boolean connectives.
- A measurement enters as `--fact name=value@source`. Two sources that disagree on the same name are retracted together. A number with no source is not a measurement.
- `pgi/packs/causal.py` is the first domain pack. It turns weather, cover, and light into path risk.
- `rules/local.json` is the editable rule store. It ships as an empty list.
- The same seed and rule version replay the same path. A new seed can reorder independent reasons and pick another legal detail line. It does not invent a missing fact.
- A contradiction is retracted and listed. The two sides are not blended into one answer.
- `pgi/corpus/records.py` is the link board. A sourced record unfolds when the ask names its subject. The record is not a settled answer. Re-asserting a forbidden law contests that law against the record. The board is the start of the reaching goal in [GOAL.md](GOAL.md). No physical constant is derived there.
- `pgi/corpus/measured.py` holds sourced numbers. Each is copied from its source as printed, and the rational is parsed from that printed text. Nothing is rounded or converted. An exact SI defining constant is an exact rational. A measured value carries the uncertainty the source quotes, under the name the source gives it. Each number becomes a board record with its source, edition, page, and retrieval date. CODATA 2022 is on the board: the seven SI defining constants and the measured core set. The PDG 2026 masses, couplings, and CKM fit are on the board with the uncertainty kind PDG prints. Clemence's 1947 Mercury perihelion residual, the GW150914 parameters from GWTC-1, and the GW170817 speed-of-gravity bound are on the board. `pgi/corpus/formulas.py` stores Planck's spectral law and the Rayleigh–Jeans law in the notation of their source, with Rayleigh–Jeans marked failed at high frequency. No constant or formula is derived there.
- An ask outside the packs stays an open gap. A predicate that is not a lessThan ray or an open-upper interval stays outside the bisection. A boolean connective whose sides the kernel can settle individually is composed by Collect_conj_eq, Collect_disj_eq, Collect_neg_eq, and imp_conv_disj: `{x. x * x <= 2 and 0 <= x}` is `atLeastAtMost(0, sqrt(2))`. A boolean connective whose sides the kernel cannot settle stays outside. Exact rational matrices have entrywise addition, multiplication, transpose, determinants, ranks, and inverses: `det([[1, 2], [3, 4]])` is `-2`, `rank([[1, 2], [2, 4]])` is `1`, and `inv([[1, 2], [3, 4]])` is `[[-2, 1], [3/2, -1/2]]`, kept only when both products are the identity. A singular inverse and a non-square determinant stay open. The characteristic polynomial is `det(tI - A)`, kept when Faddeev–LeVerrier agrees with interpolation, the polynomial annihilates the matrix, and it matches an extra evaluation point: `charpoly([[1, 2], [3, 4]])` is `x^2 - 5 * x - 2`. Real eigenvalues are the real roots of that polynomial, isolated by the algebraic-number kernel: `eigenvalues([[0, 2], [1, 0]])` is `{-sqrt(2), sqrt(2)}`. A characteristic polynomial with no real root yields the empty set. A polynomial whose resultant would exceed the kernel's degree limit stays outside. A sum of two different square roots stays that sum unless a denesting or field rule has already rewritten it. A sum of conjugate nested binomials of the same square-free integer is rewritten by squaring and checked by `real_sqrt_unique`: `sqrt(2 + sqrt(2)) + sqrt(2 - sqrt(2))` is `sqrt(4 + 2 * sqrt(2))`, and `sqrt(3 + 2 * sqrt(2)) + sqrt(3 - 2 * sqrt(2))` is `2 * sqrt(2)`. A radicand or divisor whose sign the exact algebraic or Sturm test cannot decide stays outside. A negative radicand stays open. Division by zero stays open. An inequality on free variables stays open. An unbounded quantifier stays open unless a uniform reason covers it. Division by zero stays open. Physics laws are not in this slice.

Level of detail changes what is shown. It does not recompute the claims.
